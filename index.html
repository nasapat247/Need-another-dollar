Which rule fits you?

50/30/20 — the recommended standard for most people. 70/20/10 — when charity & paying off debt are the priority: 70% for needs & wants together, 20% saving, 10% for extra debt payoff & giving. 50/50 — the aggressive pick: half to live on, half to save & invest. Not sure? Pick your focus and spin the wheel in step 1 — it lands on the rule that fits. In 50/30/20, wants & saving adjust to your actual needs; in 50/50 and 70/20/10, needs & wants combine into one bucket with a fixed target — see the Budget tab.

      Which card should you use for points?

      Points only help if you pay in full. If you carry a balance, interest wipes out every reward — use a debit card until the balance is gone. If you do pay in full, match the card to the purchase:

      🛒 Groceries → Grocery cardLook for 3–6% back at supermarkets. Your biggest weekly spend deserves your best rate.
      🍽️ Dining → Dining cardLook for 3–4% back on restaurants and takeout. Check if delivery apps count — many do.
      ✈️ Travel → Travel cardLook for 3–5x points on flights/hotels booked direct, plus no foreign transaction fee.
      💳 Everything else → Flat 2% cardOne flat-rate cash-back card (around 2% on all purchases) beats guessing. Simple wins.

      General guide only — specific cards and offers change constantly, so compare current offers before you apply, and never open a card just for points if it tempts you to overspend. Your budget split above matters 10× more than your points rate.

      When money is tight: moves that buy you time

      Budgeting assumes the month lines up. Real life doesn’t. If bills are due before payday or one bad month has you behind, these are the calls worth making — in this order. Every one has a catch, so read that part before you use it.

      1

      Move your due dates to after payday

      What it does: Lines your bills up with your money instead of against it. Most credit card companies, auto lenders, phone and utility providers let you pick a new due date — once, sometimes twice a year — for free. Spread big bills across your two paydays instead of stacking them all on the 1st.

      Say this“Hi, I’d like to change my payment due date to the [15th / day after payday]. Can you do that, and will it change anything else on my account?”

      The catch: Almost none — this is the safest move on this list, so do it first. Just know the change can take one billing cycle to kick in, and your first “long” or “short” month may make one payment look bigger or smaller than usual.

      2

      Ask your lender to move a car payment to the back of the loan

      What it does: This is called a deferment or extension. Your lender skips this month’s payment and tacks it onto the end of the loan, so you get a month of breathing room without a late fee or a credit hit — if they approve it before you miss the payment. Many lenders allow it once or twice over the life of the loan.

      Say this“I’m having a tight month but I want to stay current. Do you offer a one-month deferment that moves this payment to the end of my loan? Will it be reported as on-time?”

      The catch: Interest keeps growing. You’re not skipping the cost — that month’s interest gets added, so you’ll pay a little more over the life of the loan and finish a month later. And never just skip a payment without calling: an unapproved skip is a late payment, and that does hurt your credit.

      3

      Pause student loans with forbearance — last resort only

      What it does: For federal student loans, forbearance lets you stop making payments for a set stretch (often up to 12 months at a time) when you can’t pay. You apply through your loan servicer — don’t stop paying until it’s approved. Before you ask, check income-driven repayment first: it can lower your payment to fit your income, sometimes to $0, while keeping you on track toward forgiveness.

      Say this“What are my options to lower or pause my payment? Can you walk me through income-driven repayment first, and forbearance if that doesn’t work?”

      The catch: Interest keeps growing the whole time — and when forbearance ends, that unpaid interest can be added to your balance, so you owe interest on interest. That’s why this is a last resort, not a first move: use the shortest pause you actually need, and pay the interest during it if you possibly can.

      4

      Owe the IRS? Get on a payment plan

      What it does: The IRS lets you pay a tax bill monthly instead of all at once. Most people can set up a short-term plan (up to 180 days) or a long-term installment agreement online at irs.gov — no phone call needed. Being on an approved plan stops the worst collection actions while you pay it down. Still file your return on time even if you can’t pay — the plan handles the bill, filing late adds a separate, bigger penalty.

      Do thisGo to irs.gov, search “payment plan,” and apply online. Pick the monthly amount you can truly afford — a smaller plan you keep beats a bigger one you default on.

      The catch: Interest and a late-payment penalty keep ticking (at a reduced rate) until the balance is gone, and there’s a setup fee for long-term plans — lower if you pay by direct debit, and waived or reduced for low-income filers. A plan buys you time; it doesn’t shrink what you owe.

      The order matters: Start with due dates (free, no downside). Then call lenders before a payment is due — every option on this list is easier to get while you’re still current. Pauses and deferments buy you a month or two; use that time to fix the gap with your split and moves above, not just to push the problem forward. If several debts are behind at once, a nonprofit credit counselor (look for NFCC members) can review your whole picture for free or cheap — avoid any company that charges upfront fees to “settle” your debt.
